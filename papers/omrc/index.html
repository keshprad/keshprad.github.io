Abstract

Network failures degrade Multipath Reliable Connection (MRC) in direct-connect optical datacenters. We propose Optical MRC (OMRC), which remaps collectives and circuits, preserving 95.7% of training throughput, restoring stalled inference, and achieving 4.7× MRC’s simulated bandwidth.

Why Optical Networks Need More Than Rerouting

Different planes can have different neighbors. A healthy alternate path may be longer, or unavailable to the affected group.

In electrical datacenters, MRC spreads traffic across independent packet-switched planes. When a path fails, traffic moves to another path of the same length, losing only the failed path’s bandwidth.

Direct-connect optical datacenters replace packet switches with optical circuit switches (OCSes). Each plane connects accelerators in a ring, and different planes use different ring orders. Communication groups receive dedicated subsets of these planes.

01
Different ring orders cause detours

A pair of neighboring GPUs in one plane may be several hops apart in another. Preserving the original collective schedule adds forwarding traffic and latency.

02
Isolated groups cannot borrow connectivity

A group that loses all its planes stalls under MRC, even when another communication group still has healthy planes.

Core idea: Adapt the collective schedule to the surviving network; adapt the network itself when the group is disconnected.

Design of OMRC

A topology-aware control layer extends MRC across collective scheduling and optical circuit allocation.

1

Match channels to physical rings

Each channel defines a collective schedule. OMRC matches the logical ring order to a physical plane so scheduled neighbor exchanges take one direct hop. Ring collectives produce the same result for any ring order.

2

Remap at collective boundaries

After a failure, OMRC can move a channel onto an intact plane within its group and rebalance data across channels. Connected Alltoall groups keep their schedule and use transport rerouting.

3

Reallocate planes across groups

If a group cannot route its required flows, the controller reconfigures the OCS to obtain planes from another group. It updates allocations, and the transport rebuilds its path sets.

When does OMRC remap a channel?

For an Allreduce channel c, let hf be the shortest available path for flow f. The hop-count spread is:

Δhc = maxf ∈ c hf − minf ∈ c hf

If the spread exceeds a threshold τ, OMRC looks for a plane in the group’s allocation whose topology-matched schedule has spread at most τ. If one exists, OMRC remaps at a collective boundary; otherwise, it keeps the schedule and uses transport rerouting. The experiments use τ = 0 on the testbed and τ = 2 in simulation.

Prototype & Experimental Setup

The prototype runs on four hosts with one NVIDIA L40 GPU each. Each host has four 100 Gb/s RDMA ports connected through a 32 × 32 Polatis 6000n OCS.

Collectives use MSCCL++ with RoCEv2 transport. Both OMRC and the MRC baseline use software-emulated multipath transport with GPU store-and-forward because the testbed NICs lack the required forwarding capability.

Training and inference use Qwen1.5-MoE-A2.7B-Chat: 14.3B total parameters, 24 layers, and 60 experts per layer with top-4 routing, using WikiText-2 tokens.

Large-scale experiments extend ASTRA-sim’s congestion-aware analytical network backend to model direct-connect optical planes, multipath forwarding, channel remapping, and circuit reconfiguration.

Results

Measured training and inference on the optical testbed, followed by failure scaling in simulation.

Temporary link failure during training

Four-rank tensor and expert parallelism (TP4/EP4) spans two planes with four channels. A link is cut at 25 seconds and restored 40 seconds later.

MRC reroutes the affected flow. OMRC instead remaps the affected channel, reducing mean hop count from 1.125 to 1.0.

4.97 vs. 4.00 GB/s OMRC vs. MRC · 24% gain

Alltoall bandwidth differs by less than 4% between the two policies.

Takeaway: Collective remapping preserves 95.7% of healthy training throughput during the degraded phase.

Permanent link failure during disaggregated inference

Prefill runs on ranks [0, 1] and decode on [2, 3]. Each stage uses TP2/EP2 with one plane. Each KV-cache transfer pair starts with three planes.

The sole TP/EP plane is disabled after 80 seconds. MRC stalls. OMRC reconfigures the OCS to move a plane from the KV-cache transfer pairs and restore TP/EP connectivity.

Takeaway: Plane reallocation restores 99.3% of pre-failure inference throughput where transport rerouting alone cannot recover connectivity.

Scaling under compounding link failures

ASTRA-sim evaluates 64 MB Allreduce while varying rank count and plane count. Links are randomly selected and disabled; each configuration is sampled four times, and the plots show the means. The remapping threshold is τ = 2.

Takeaway: With four planes and four failed links, OMRC achieves 1.74× MRC’s Allreduce bandwidth at 16 ranks and 4.74× at 64 ranks. These are simulated collective bandwidth results.

Key Contributions

01

Expose the limits of transport-only recovery

Unequal path lengths and dedicated group allocations make direct-connect optical networks different from the packet-switched planes MRC was designed for.

02

Coordinate collective schedules and optical topology

OMRC combines topology-aware channel remapping within a group with OCS plane reallocation across groups, supported by per-rank sidecars and a central controller.

03

Evaluate application recovery and collective scaling

An MSCCL++ prototype demonstrates training and inference resilience on a four-GPU optical testbed. ASTRA-sim experiments evaluate bandwidth under compounding failures at larger group sizes.
